Earth Science Reviewer: The Restless Planet

THE LAYERS OF THE EARTH
The Earth is made of layers that differ in composition and in physical state. The outermost layer is the crust, a thin shell of solid rock. Continental crust is thicker and less dense, and is made mostly of granite. Oceanic crust is thinner and denser, and is made mostly of basalt. Beneath the crust lies the mantle, the thickest layer of the Earth, made of hot rock rich in iron and magnesium. Although the mantle is mostly solid, part of the upper mantle can flow very slowly over long periods of time. The crust and the rigid top of the mantle together form the lithosphere. Below the lithosphere is the asthenosphere, a weaker, partly molten zone of the upper mantle on which the lithosphere moves. At the centre of the Earth is the core. The outer core is liquid iron and nickel, and its movement produces the Earth's magnetic field. The inner core is solid iron and nickel; it stays solid despite the extreme heat because the pressure there is enormous.

Scientists learned about these layers mostly by studying seismic waves. When waves from an earthquake pass through the Earth, they bend, speed up, slow down or stop depending on the material they travel through. S-waves cannot travel through liquid, and the fact that they do not pass through the outer core is the main evidence that the outer core is liquid.

PLATE TECTONICS
The theory of plate tectonics states that the lithosphere is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates, which move slowly over the asthenosphere. Plates move only a few centimetres each year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Convection currents in the mantle, where hot material rises and cooler material sinks, help drive this motion. Two other forces also move plates: ridge push, where new crust at a mid-ocean ridge pushes plates apart, and slab pull, where a sinking plate drags the rest of the plate behind it.

The idea began with continental drift, proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912. Wegener argued that the continents were once joined in a single supercontinent he called Pangaea. His evidence included the matching shapes of the coastlines of South America and Africa, the same fossils found on continents now separated by oceans, matching rock layers and mountain ranges across oceans, and signs of ancient glaciers in places that are warm today. Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents, so many scientists rejected his idea. In the 1960s, the discovery of seafloor spreading supplied the missing mechanism. At mid-ocean ridges, magma rises, cools and forms new oceanic crust, which pushes older crust away from the ridge. Rocks on the seafloor record the Earth's magnetic field as they cool, and the matching stripes of magnetic reversals on both sides of a ridge show that the seafloor is spreading.

PLATE BOUNDARIES
Most earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain ranges are found along plate boundaries. There are three main kinds.

At a divergent boundary, plates move apart. On the ocean floor this forms a mid-ocean ridge, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. On land it forms a rift valley, such as the East African Rift.

At a convergent boundary, plates move toward each other. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks beneath the continental plate in a process called subduction. Subduction forms a deep ocean trench and a chain of volcanoes on the continent. When two oceanic plates meet, one subducts beneath the other and forms a trench and a chain of volcanic islands called an island arc. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples and folds upward into high mountains; the Himalayas formed this way when the Indian Plate collided with the Eurasian Plate.

At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other horizontally. Crust is neither created nor destroyed, but the plates lock and then slip suddenly, causing earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary.

The Philippines lies where the Philippine Sea Plate and the Eurasian Plate meet, and it is part of the Pacific Ring of Fire, a belt around the Pacific Ocean with many active volcanoes and frequent earthquakes. The Philippine Trench, east of the islands, is a deep trench formed by subduction. The Philippine Fault Zone runs through much of the country.

EARTHQUAKES
An earthquake is the sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy stored in rocks. Stress builds up along a fault, a break in the crust where rocks move past each other. When the stress becomes greater than the strength of the rocks, they slip and release energy as seismic waves. The point inside the Earth where the slip begins is the focus, or hypocenter. The point on the surface directly above the focus is the epicenter.

There are two main kinds of seismic waves. Body waves travel through the inside of the Earth. P-waves, or primary waves, are the fastest seismic waves and arrive first; they push and pull the rock in the direction the wave travels, and they can pass through solids, liquids and gases. S-waves, or secondary waves, are slower; they move the rock up and down or side to side, at right angles to the direction the wave travels, and they can pass only through solids. Surface waves travel along the Earth's surface; they are the slowest, but they usually cause the most damage.

A seismograph records seismic waves. By comparing the arrival times of P-waves and S-waves at three or more seismic stations, scientists can locate the epicenter; this method is called triangulation. Magnitude measures the energy an earthquake releases, and today it is usually reported on the moment magnitude scale. Each whole number on the scale means about 32 times more energy. Intensity describes how strongly the shaking is felt and how much damage it causes at a particular place. In the Philippines, PHIVOLCS reports intensity on the PHIVOLCS Earthquake Intensity Scale, which runs from Intensity I to Intensity X.

An earthquake under the sea can lift or drop the seafloor and push up the water above it, producing a tsunami, a series of very long ocean waves. A sudden drop of the sea level that exposes the seafloor can be a natural warning that a tsunami is coming, and people should move to high ground at once. During an earthquake, the safest action is to drop, cover and hold on.

VOLCANOES
A volcano is an opening in the crust through which magma, gases and ash escape. Magma is molten rock below the surface; once it reaches the surface it is called lava. The amount of silica in magma controls its viscosity, or resistance to flow. Magma with a lot of silica is thick and traps gas, so it tends to produce explosive eruptions. Magma with little silica is runny and lets gas escape, so it tends to produce gentle, flowing eruptions.

There are three main types of volcanoes. A shield volcano is wide with gentle slopes, built by many flows of runny basaltic lava; Mauna Loa in Hawaii is an example. A cinder cone is a small, steep volcano built from pieces of lava and ash that fall around a single vent. A stratovolcano, also called a composite volcano, is tall and cone-shaped and is built from alternating layers of lava and ash; its eruptions are often explosive. Mayon Volcano in Albay is a stratovolcano famous for its nearly perfect cone.

Explosive eruptions can produce pyroclastic flows, fast-moving clouds of hot gas, ash and rock that rush down the slopes, and lahars, mudflows of volcanic debris mixed with water. The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo was one of the largest eruptions of the twentieth century; its ash and gases spread around the world and cooled the global climate slightly for about two years. A volcano is called active if it has erupted in recorded history or shows signs of activity, and inactive if it has no record of eruption. Taal Volcano in Batangas is an active volcano that sits inside a lake.

ROCKS AND THE ROCK CYCLE
Rocks are grouped into three types by how they form. Igneous rocks form when magma or lava cools and hardens. Intrusive igneous rocks, such as granite, cool slowly underground and have large crystals. Extrusive igneous rocks, such as basalt and pumice, cool quickly at the surface and have small crystals or none. Sedimentary rocks form when sediments, pieces of rock, shells and other materials, are deposited in layers and then compacted and cemented together; sandstone, shale and limestone are examples, and most fossils are found in sedimentary rocks. Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure without melting; marble forms from limestone, and slate forms from shale.

The rock cycle is the continuous process by which rocks change from one type into another. Weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces. Mechanical weathering breaks rocks without changing their chemical make-up, for example when water freezes in cracks and expands. Chemical weathering changes the minerals in the rock, for example when acidic rainwater dissolves limestone. Erosion is the movement of weathered material by water, wind, ice or gravity, and deposition is the dropping of that material in a new place.

THE ATMOSPHERE AND WEATHER
The atmosphere is the layer of gases around the Earth. It is about 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour. It has four main layers. The troposphere is the lowest layer, where almost all weather happens, and temperature falls as height increases. The stratosphere lies above it and contains the ozone layer, which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun. The mesosphere is the coldest layer, and most meteors burn up there. The thermosphere is the highest main layer; it is very thin, and the auroras happen there.

Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time, while climate is the average weather of a region over many years. A tropical cyclone is a large storm that forms over warm ocean water and spins around a calm centre called the eye. In the Philippines, tropical cyclones are called typhoons when they are strong, and PAGASA, the national weather agency, gives each one a local name and raises Tropical Cyclone Wind Signals that warn people about the expected wind strength in their area.

The water cycle moves water around the Earth. The Sun heats water in oceans, lakes and rivers, and it turns into water vapour through evaporation. Plants also release water vapour through transpiration. As the vapour rises and cools, it turns back into tiny droplets through condensation and forms clouds. When the droplets grow heavy, they fall as precipitation, such as rain, snow or hail. The water then collects in bodies of water or soaks into the ground as groundwater, and the cycle begins again.
